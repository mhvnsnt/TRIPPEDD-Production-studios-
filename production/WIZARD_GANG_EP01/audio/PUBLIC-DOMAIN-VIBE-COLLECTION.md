# PUBLIC DOMAIN VIBE COLLECTION
### A creative director's shopping list for TRIPPEDD / Wizard Gang

**Rule zero (already standing):** Composition PD ≠ recording PD. We replay and
interpolate — we never lift someone else's recording. Mozart died 1791; his notes
are ours. Somebody's 1998 orchestra recording of those notes is NOT.

**What's NOT here:** Grimm, Dracula, Sherlock Holmes, Alice in Wonderland —
mined to death, everybody's done them, we're not doing them.

**US public domain lines (2026):** compositions/books published 1930 or earlier ·
sound recordings released 1926 or earlier · films released 1930 or earlier.

---

## 1. DARK / RITUAL CLASSICAL — the original trip music

### Berlioz — Symphonie Fantastique (1830)
**What:** Five-movement symphony about an artist's opium dream. He poisons
himself, hallucinates a witches' sabbath, hears his lover's theme twisted into a
demonic dance. This is literally a 19th-century bad trip set to orchestra.
**Why it fits:** It's THE original psychedelic composition — 130 years before
the 60s. March to the Scaffold + Dream of a Witches' Sabbath is the darkest
finale in classical music.
**How we use it:** The Witches' Sabbath movement is a villain-entrance goldmine.
Replay the Dies Irae chant from the finale as a recurring Wizard Gang motif —
every time something demonic happens, that chant creeps in. Character theme
material for whoever the darkest wizard ends up being.

### Mussorgsky — Night on Bald Mountain (1867)
**What:** Witches gather on a mountaintop at midnight, Chernobog presides,
dawn scatters them. Raw, ugly, pagan.
**Why it fits:** Ritual gathering music. Disney used it in Fantasia but the
notes are PD — and Disney's version isn't the only way to play it.
**How we use it:** Council-of-wizards scenes. Any time the gang does something
ritualistic, this is the harmonic language — not the Disney arrangement, our
own dirty replay. Sample-flip the opening for a bump: 10 seconds of dread.

### Saint-Saëns — Danse Macabre (1874)
**What:** Death tunes his fiddle at midnight, skeletons dance until dawn.
Xylophone = rattling bones (it was written FOR that).
**Why it fits:** Playful evil. Not heavy-dread like Mussorgsky — this is evil
having FUN. That's a different flavor we don't have yet.
**How we use it:** Fake commercial music. Anything comedic-evil — a leprechaun
doing something shady, a trickster beat. The xylophone skeleton-rattle is an
instant SFX-musical hybrid.

### Scriabin — Piano Sonata No. 9 "Black Mass" (1913)
**What:** Scriabin was a theosophist who thought music could end the world.
This sonata is his evil-ritual piece — clusters, tritones, genuinely
uncomfortable harmony.
**Why it fits:** Occult composer, occult piece, wizard show. The harmony is
alien — it doesn't resolve the way your ear expects, which is exactly the
trippy unease we want in weird scenes.
**How we use it:** Transitional unease. Under dialogue scenes that need to feel
WRONG without being loud. Study the chord voicings for our "richer chords"
expansion — Scriabin was stacking extensions before jazz was.

### Liszt — Totentanz (1849, rev. 1859)
**What:** Variations on the Dies Irae for piano and orchestra. Demonic piano
showpiece.
**Why it fits:** Piano-forward darkness — matches our current piano-led
orchestration. The Dies Irae theme again (notice a pattern — it's the meme
that ate classical music).
**How we use it:** Kiko-adjacent darkness for a different character. If Kiko
gets the mournful D minor, somebody gets the demonic Dies Irae piano.

## 2. EARLY BLUES & JAZZ — swagger with dirt under its nails

### Blind Lemon Jefferson — 1926 Paramount sides
**What:** "Long Lonesome Blues," "Got the Blues," "Booster Blues." Solo voice
+ guitar, recorded 1926 — the RECORDINGS are PD as of Jan 1, 2026, not just
the compositions. First of his ~100 tracks, all on Paramount.
**Why it fits:** This is the rawest commercial blues ever recorded. One blind
man, one guitar, total command. The swagger-to-sorrow ratio is exactly the
Wizard Gang register.
**How we use it:** BOTH the recordings (PD — we can actually use them) and
replays. A Blind Lemon guitar lick under a scene does more work than any
synth pad. Character theme for the drifter/trickster archetype.

### King Oliver's Creole Jazz Band (1923, feat. young Louis Armstrong)
**What:** The 1923 Gennett/Okeh sides — Armstrong's first recordings, second
cornet to Oliver. Recordings PD (1923 = PD since 2023).
**Why it fits:** The actual birth of the jazz solo is on these records.
Armstrong's breaks on "Dippermouth Blues" are the first time anyone played
like THAT. Swagger invented in real time.
**How we use it:** The actual recordings in period scenes. Replay the
collective-improvisation texture (everyone soloing at once, somehow working)
for chaotic group scenes — the gang arguing, a bar fight, a ritual going
sideways.

### Jelly Roll Morton — 1923–1926 sides
**What:** Self-proclaimed inventor of jazz (he wasn't, but the confidence is
the point). Piano-led, 1923–1926 recordings PD.
**Why it fits:** Morton talked more shit than any musician before rap existed.
The piano is the lead voice — maps directly onto our piano-forward
orchestration.
**How we use it:** Study his left hand. Morton's stride patterns under a right
hand that won't sit still is a piano vocabulary we can steal for Kiko's
expansion — richer chords, moving bass, same piano.

### Ma Rainey & Bessie Smith — 1923–1926 sides
**What:** The mothers of the blues. "Downhearted Blues" (Bessie, 1923) sold
750,000 copies. Recordings PD.
**Why it fits:** Power vocals over minimal backing — the template for every
belter since. The emotional directness is the opposite of our choir-pad
subtlety, which makes it a contrast weapon.
**How we use it:** A Bessie-style vocal phrase (replayed, new melody) as a
soul anchor in an episode about loss. Also: the 1920s Black vaudeville-blues
circuit is the direct ancestor of the chitlin' circuit is the direct ancestor
of rap touring — that's a lineage worth naming in-show.

## 3. REAL OCCULT TEXTS — the actual grimoires

### The Lesser Key of Solomon (Crowley/Mathers ed., 1904)
**What:** 72 demons, each with a seal/sigil, ranks, summoning instructions.
The Ars Goetia is the famous part.
**Why it fits:** Real demon seals designed for visual use. These aren't made-up
fantasy runes — they're 400-year-old graphic design, and they look incredible.
**How we use it:** VISUALS. The seals go on title cards, on wizards' robes, in
background art, in fake commercials for "demon insurance." Never explain them
— let the audience google it and fall down the hole.

### Eliphas Levi — Transcendental Magic (1896 English trans.)
**What:** The book that invented the modern occult aesthetic. The Baphomet
image (goat head, "solve et coagula") comes from here.
**Why it fits:** Every wizard-with-a-goat-head image in pop culture traces to
this one illustration. It's the visual source code.
**How we use it:** Art direction reference. The Baphomet composition — as
above so below, one hand up one hand down — is a pose library for wizard
character art.

### The Kybalion (1908)
**What:** Seven Hermetic principles. "As above, so below." Mentalism,
correspondence, vibration, polarity, rhythm, cause and effect, gender.
**Why it fits:** Trippy philosophy in plain language. "The universe is mental"
is a show bible in one sentence.
**How we use it:** DIALOGUE. A wizard explaining the Kybalion's principles in
plain speech is an exposition scene that doesn't feel like exposition —
because the ideas are genuinely mind-bending. "As above, so below" is a
tagline waiting to happen.

### Agrippa — Three Books of Occult Philosophy (1651 English)
**What:** The Renaissance wizard's textbook. Planetary correspondences, angel
names, number magic.
**Why it fits:** It's the worldbuilding document — a complete system where
every planet has a color, a metal, an angel, a day. That's a magic system with
500 years of playtesting.
**How we use it:** Show bible material. When we need rules for how magic works,
steal Agrippa's correspondences instead of inventing from nothing — they're
weirder and more specific than anything we'd make up.

### The Sacred Magic of Abramelin the Mage (Mathers trans., 1898)
**What:** An 18-month ritual to contact your Holy Guardian Angel. The real
deal — Crowley tried it.
**Why it fits:** The "long ritual" structure is a story engine: preparation,
isolation, the thing you talk to at the end.
**How we use it:** Episode structure. A character doing a version of the
Abramelin operation — the isolation, the daily practice, the payoff — is a
full episode arc wearing a 600-year-old coat.

## 4. WEIRD FICTION (pre-Lovecraft, pre-1930) — cosmic dread before it was cool

### William Hope Hodgson — The House on the Borderland (1908), The Night Land (1912)
**What:** Cosmic horror before Lovecraft. The Night Land is set millions of
years in the future after the sun dies — humanity survives in one last
pyramid while Things watch from the dark.
**Why it fits:** The scale is bigger than Lovecraft — deeper time, stranger
monsters, more romance. And nobody's mined it. No movies, no memes, untouched.
**How we use it:** Setting bible for any "far future" or "dead world" beat.
The Last Redoubt (humanity's final pyramid) is a location design waiting to
happen.

### Arthur Machen — The Great God Pan (1894)
**What:** A woman sees past the veil during brain surgery and it destroys her.
Pan is not a cute satyr here — he's the god of the thing underneath reality.
**Why it fits:** "Seeing too much" as horror. The veil-between-worlds concept
is the Wizard Gang premise wearing Victorian clothes.
**How we use it:** The "veil" language. Machen's version of the reveal — beauty
that curdles into wrongness — is the tone for our trippiest visual sequences.

### Algernon Blackwood — The Willows (1907)
**What:** Two men canoeing on the Danube realize the willows are watching
them. Nothing jumps out. The dread just builds until the landscape itself is
the monster.
**Why it fits:** Horror without a monster — the environment is the threat.
That's a harder trick and a more TRIPPEDD one.
**How we use it:** Pacing reference for suspense scenes. Study how Blackwood
does 40 pages of "nothing happening" that terrifies — that's the pier scene's
older brother.

### Lord Dunsany — The Gods of Pegana (1905)
**What:** An entirely invented pantheon, written in Bible-English. Gods who
play with worlds and get bored.
**Why it fits:** Original mythology built from nothing — the proof that you
can invent gods and make them feel ancient. The prose style (faux-scripture)
is a voice we can borrow.
**How we use it:** In-world religious texts. When a wizard quotes scripture,
it should sound like Dunsany — invented, archaic, slightly off.

## 5. THEATER — horror plays, Black Broadway, vaudeville bones

### Grand Guignol (Paris, 1897–1962)
**What:** The horror theater. Graphic stage violence, medical horror,
madness — with a house doctor for fainters. Programmed as "hot and cold
showers": a horror play, then a comedy, then horror again.
**Why it fits:** The hot-and-cold programming IS the TRIPPEDD episode
structure — horror beat, comedy beat, horror beat. They invented our format
in 1897.
**How we use it:** Structural bible. Study the programming rhythm — how long
each "shower" lasted, how they released tension. Our episode pacing should
steal this deliberately.

### Shuffle Along (1921) — Sissle & Blake
**What:** The all-Black Broadway musical that launched the Harlem Renaissance
on stage. "I'm Just Wild About Harry." 504 performances. PD in the US
(confirmed via IMSLP).
**Why it fits:** Black excellence on Broadway in 1921 — against everything.
The music is the bridge between ragtime and jazz, which is the bridge to
everything we do.
**How we use it:** Replay the songbook for period scenes. "I'm Just Wild About
Harry" as a character's ringtone-equivalent — an anachronism that tells you
everything about them. Study the orchestrations for our pit-band vocabulary.

### Bert Williams (d. 1922) — Black vaudeville's biggest star
**What:** The first Black superstar of American theater. Headlined the
Ziegfeld Follies. His 1920s recordings are PD.
**Why it fits:** Williams played the "coon" stereotype on stage and
subverted it from inside — the saddest man in the funniest mask. That's a
character study in one biography.
**How we use it:** Character reference for the tragic clown archetype. His
signature song "Nobody" (1905) is a PD masterclass in doing devastating
material inside a comedy frame.

### The cakewalk (origin: 19th century, PD)
**What:** Started as enslaved Black people satirizing white formal dance —
then white audiences stole it without getting the joke. The history is the
content warning AND the point.
**Why it fits:** It's the original "they took our thing and missed the point"
— which is a whole TRIPPEDD thesis in one dance.
**How we use it:** Handle with care and knowledge. The cakewalk as a visual
motif in a scene about appropriation — the characters know the history even
if the audience has to catch up.

## 6. FILM & ANIMATION — the original trippy visuals

### Méliès — A Trip to the Moon (1902), The Haunted Castle (1896), The Impossible Voyage (1904)
**What:** The first special effects. Hand-painted color, substitution splices,
multiple exposures. The moon with a rocket in its eye is the most famous
image in early film.
**Why it fits:** THE original trippy visuals. Every dissolve, every
transformation, every impossible set — Méliès did it with scissors and
paint 120 years ago.
**How we use it:** Direct visual quotation. A Méliès-style moon face in a
transition, hand-painted color flashes, the theatrical proscenium framing.
Our "trippy cards" are Méliès' descendants — say so on screen.

### Winsor McCay — Gertie the Dinosaur (1914), Little Nemo (1911)
**What:** McCay was a vaudeville chalk-talk artist who taught himself
animation. Gertie is the first character animation — she has a personality.
Little Nemo's Slumberland is the trippiest comic strip ever drawn.
**Why it fits:** Slumberland (1905–1914, PD) is a kid's dream-world drawn with
Art Nouveau precision — palaces that morph, landscapes that breathe. It's the
visual ancestor of everything psychedelic in comics.
**How we use it:** Slumberland panel compositions as storyboard reference for
dream/trip sequences. Gertie's personality-animation as the bar for our
character acting — if a 1914 dinosaur has more soul than our rig, we're
doing it wrong.

### Early Fleischer — Out of the Inkwell / Koko the Clown (1918–1921)
**What:** A live-action clown hand reaches into an inkwell and pulls out an
animated clown. The boundary between real and drawn is the whole gag.
**Why it fits:** The real/drawn boundary play is pure Wizard Gang — our show
lives on that line.
**How we use it:** The inkwell gag as a transition device. A hand reaching
into the frame and pulling animation out of nothing — that's a bump, a title
card, a scene transition, all in one 1918 idea.

### Häxan: Witchcraft Through the Ages (1922)
**What:** Part documentary, part horror film. Christensen dramatizes medieval
witch trials with genuinely disturbing imagery — then argues the "witches"
were mentally ill women failed by their society.
**Why it fits:** Witches + social commentary + silent-era imagery that still
disturbs. The thesis (society makes monsters, then punishes them) is a
Wizard Gang theme wearing 1922 clothes.
**How we use it:** Visual reference for witch/wizard imagery — the
demon-faces, the sabbath scenes. And the structure (dramatization +
argument) is a template for our fake documentaries.

### Nosferatu (1922) / Caligari (1920) / Phantom of the Opera (1925 film)
**What:** The silent horror canon. All PD. Orlok's shadow on the stairs,
Caligari's painted sets, Chaney's unmasking.
**Why it fits:** Everyone knows them which means we have to use them SLANT —
not the famous shots, the weird corners. Caligari's painted shadows (not real
shadows — PAINTED ON) is a whole art-direction philosophy.
**How we use it:** Caligari's painted-light technique as a design rule: in
trippy scenes, shadows don't have to obey physics. Orlok's creeping shadow as
a transition wipe.

## 7. WRESTLING & CARNIVAL — the underlying theme's source code

### The carnival wrestling era (1890s–1920s)
**What:** Wrestling came from carnivals. The "hooker" took all challengers;
the barker talked the crowd into the tent. Frank Gotch vs. Hackenschmidt
(1908) was the Ali-Frazier of its day — 30,000 people in Chicago.
**Why it fits:** This is wrestling's origin story and it's pure theater —
worked matches, real shooters as enforcers, the line between sport and show
that wrestling still walks.
**How we use it:** The barker's pitch as a dialogue style — every wrestling
promo since is a carnival barker's pitch with better lighting. A Kiko promo
cut like a 1910 barker ("step RIGHT up") is comedy AND history.

### Eugen Sandow (d. 1925) — physical culture
**What:** The first bodybuilding superstar. Toured vaudeville, posed as
classical statues, sold chest-expanders. PD photos everywhere.
**Why it fits:** The strongman-as-living-statue is a visual — oiled muscle
as Greek art. The swag is a century old.
**How we use it:** Character design reference for the powerhouse archetype.
Sandow's poses (as Hercules, as Achilles) are a pose library.

### Farmer Burns (Martin Burns, d. 1937) — catch wrestling
**What:** The trainer of Gotch. Claimed he could take any man in minutes.
Wrote mail-order wrestling courses — the original fitness influencer.
**Why it fits:** The mail-order course (PD texts exist) is the 1910s version
of a YouTube tutorial empire. Hustle culture before it had a name.
**How we use it:** Fake commercial material. A "Farmer Burns' School of
Wrestling" parody ad writes itself — and the real course text is PD to quote.

## 8. HARLEM RENAISSANCE & URBAN CULTURE — the language and the rooms

### Langston Hughes — The Weary Blues (1926), Fine Clothes to the Jew (1927)
**What:** PD. Blues poems — the first time the blues got treated as serious
poetry. "The Weary Blues" puts you in the room with the piano player.
**Why it fits:** Hughes wrote the blues as literature without sanding off
the edges. The voice — direct, musical, Black — is a register our show
needs in its DNA.
**How we use it:** Epigraphs. A Hughes stanza as a title card. His blues-poem
form (repetition with a turn) as a dialogue rhythm for musical characters.

### Zora Neale Hurston — "Sweat" (1926), early folklore work
**What:** PD for pre-1931 publications. "Sweat" is a perfect short story —
a Black woman, a no-good husband, a snake, and the most cold-blooded ending
in American fiction.
**Why it fits:** Hurston documented the actual speech, stories, and hoodoo of
the Black South. Her ear for dialogue is unmatched — people talking like
people actually talk.
**How we use it:** Dialogue bible. When we need Southern Black speech that
isn't caricature, Hurston's ear is the reference. Her hoodoo documentation
(also PD-era) feeds the show's folk-magic vocabulary.

### Jean Toomer — Cane (1923)
**What:** PD. Part poetry, part prose, part play — a formally experimental
book about the Black South that invented its own structure.
**Why it fits:** It refuses to be one thing — exactly our show's problem and
solution. The mixed form (poem into story into dialogue) is a structural
permission slip.
**How we use it:** Structural reference. Cane's formlessness-as-form is the
argument for our Adult Swim weave — poem, scene, song, fake ad, no apology.

### Wallace Thurman — The Blacker the Berry (1929), Fire!! magazine (1926)
**What:** PD. Thurman was the Renaissance's bad boy — Fire!! was the
"fuck you" magazine, one issue, too queer and too raw for the respectability
crowd.
**Why it fits:** The anti-respectability wing. Fire!! published what the
mainstream Black press wouldn't — that's our lane.
**How we use it:** Attitude reference. When the show needs to be the thing
the respectable version wouldn't air, Fire!! is the patron saint.

### The slang record (NOT Calloway's dictionary — 1938, NOT PD)
**What:** Calloway's Hepster's Dictionary is 1938 — not public domain, don't
touch the text. BUT the slang itself was documented in PD sources: Billy
Rowe's "The Notebook" column in the Pittsburgh Courier (1920s–30s, pre-1931
issues PD), and 1920s Black newspapers generally.
**Why it fits:** The language is the culture. "Hep," "jive," "the Apple" —
Calloway documented it, but the streets made it, and the streets' version
is in PD newsprint.
**How we use it:** Period-accurate slang from PD newspaper archives for any
1920s-set material. The Pittsburgh Courier archive is a dialogue mine.

### Rent parties & the numbers game (historical, not copyrightable)
**What:** Rent parties (pay at the door, piano player all night, fish fry in
the kitchen) were the economic engine of Harlem nightlife. The numbers game
(policy) was the community bank before it was the mob's.
**Why it fits:** The economics of the culture — how the music got paid for.
A rent party is a venue, a plot, and a vibe in one historical fact.
**How we use it:** Setting bible. A rent-party scene is historically dense —
music, food, money, community, danger — and it actually happened thousands
of times. No invention needed.

## HOW THIS GETS USED — the pipeline

1. **Study** — each item above feeds the MUSIC-REFERENCE-PORTFOLIO.md playbook
   as we work through them (same format as the Lacrimosa study).
2. **Replay, don't lift** — PD composition? Replay it ourselves. PD recording?
   We can use it, but replays usually fit the mix better.
3. **Credit the source** — "after Berlioz" in the cue sheet. The audience that
   gets it will love us for it; the audience that doesn't will just feel it.
4. **Build the pile** — every studied item becomes a tool in the box: a chord
   vocabulary, a visual motif, a dialogue rhythm, a structural trick.
