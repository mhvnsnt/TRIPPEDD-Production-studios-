# KIKO PIER SONG — Score Study & Orchestration Expansion Plan

**Date:** 2026-10-11
**Purpose:** Study reference scores matching Kiko's pier song energy (moonlit, theatrical, memorial),
then recommend an expanded orchestration.

## HARD CONSTRAINT (owner directive 2026-10-11)

**ADDITIVE ONLY.** The existing three voices — Piano (GM 0) + Strings (GM 48) +
Choir Pad (GM 88) — stay EXACTLY as they are. Nothing is replaced, revoiced, or removed.
New instruments and richer harmonies layer ON TOP. Richer chords means extending
the existing triads (Dm → Dm9) in the NEW layers, never rewriting the piano part.

**Key:** D minor | **Tempo:** 60 BPM | **Meter:** 4/4 | **Duration:** 2:31

### What's missing (the expansion palette)
- **Bass:** No double bass, no cello low register, no bass guitar/synth bass. Low end is open (reserved for foghorn SFX in the scene version, but the full song can use it).
- **Brass:** No trumpets, horns, trombones, tuba. Zero brass weight in choruses.
- **Woodwinds:** No flute, oboe, clarinet, bassoon. No breathy color.
- **Percussion:** No timpani, no tam-tam, no suspended cymbal, no snare. The outro BUTTON is currently just a piano/strings slam — no percussive impact.
- **Harp:** No glissandi, no arpeggiated textures under the piano.
- **Solo strings:** No solo cello, no solo violin. Everything is ensemble pad.
- **Keyboard color:** No pipe organ, no celesta, no Rhodes. Piano is the only keyboard.
- **Extended harmony:** All chords are triads or 7ths. No 9ths, 11ths, add9 colors.

## PART 2: Reference Studies

### CLASSICAL

#### 1. Mozart — Requiem in D minor, K. 626 (1791)
**Key match:** D minor — the SAME key as Kiko's song. Mozart's "tragic key."
**Why it fits:** A memorial mass written as the composer was dying. The Lacrimosa
("tearful") builds from a delicate mourning melody to a devastating choral climax —
the exact dynamic arc of Kiko's song (quiet verse → swelling chorus → hard button).
The Introitus opens in darkness and never fully resolves, like Kiko alone on the pier.

**Orchestration detail to steal:** Mozart deliberately OMITTED high woodwinds
(no flutes, no oboes) and used basset horns instead, darkening the entire orchestral
color. **For Kiko:** when adding woodwinds, skip the bright flute/oboe and go straight
to bass clarinet and bassoon in the low register — keep the darkness. Add them in
Verse 2 (the "darker" verse) as a low counterline under the piano.

**Chord idea:** The Requiem's opening Dm is voiced with the 9th (E) sighing above it
in the strings. **Additive layer:** have the new divisi violins hold Dm(add9) —
D-F-A-E — over the piano's plain Dm triad in the intro and bridge.

#### 2. Górecki — Symphony No. 3, "Symphony of Sorrowful Songs," Op. 36 (1976)
**Why it fits:** Three slow laments for solo voice over strings. A mother mourning
her son (movement 3), a prisoner praying on a Gestapo cell wall (movement 2).
It is memorial music at its most stripped-back — and Kiko's bridge ("I wonder if
father's watching") is the same emotional territory: a child addressing an absent parent.

**Orchestration detail to steal:** Górecki writes strings in TEN divided parts
(16/16/12/12/8, each split in two) — a massive, slowly breathing string carpet.
The dynamics almost never exceed mezzo-forte; the power comes from mass and patience,
not volume. Trombones appear for only a few bars in the entire 54-minute work —
when they enter, it lands like a thunderclap because of the restraint before it.
**For Kiko:** divide the existing string pad concept into high divisi violins
(sustaining 9th/11th extensions) and low divisi cellos/basses (root + fifth pedal)
in the choruses. Add a single pair of muted trombones ONLY in the final chorus
(2:10–2:28) — nowhere else. Their entrance should feel like the sky opening.

**Chord idea:** Górecki's harmony is modal and static — chords held for minutes.
**Additive layer:** in the bridge (1:58–2:10), have divided violas hold a Gm(add9)
for the full 12 seconds without moving, while the piano plays its sparse figure.
Stillness as orchestration.

#### 3. Barber — Adagio for Strings, Op. 11 (1936)
**Key:** B-flat minor. **Why it fits:** America's mourning music (FDR, JFK, 9/11).
A single arch: one melodic line that climbs, intensifies through layered counterpoint,
hits an unbearable climax, then falls into silence. Kiko's song has the same shape
on a smaller scale — and the outro BUTTON is Barber's "two final chords" gesture.

**Orchestration detail to steal:** The entire piece is STRINGS ONLY — no brass,
no woodwinds, no percussion. Barber proves that divided strings alone can carry
memorial weight. The climax works because every string part has an independent
singing line (it's contrapuntal, not just block chords). **For Kiko:** in the
final chorus, give the new string layers independent moving lines (not just pads) —
a cello counter-melody that answers Kiko's vocal phrase, violas pulsing gently
underneath. Counterpoint, not just volume.

**Chord idea:** Barber's climax chord is a massive dominant with added dissonances
that resolve by half-steps. **Additive layer:** at the outro BUTTON (2:28), stack
the new brass + strings on a Dm(maj9) — D-F-A-C#-E — a dark, luminous crunch that
resolves the whole song's tension in one hit.

#### 4. Fauré — Requiem, Op. 48 (1888)
**Why it fits:** The gentlest requiem ever written — Fauré said it was about
rest and consolation, not judgment. The "In Paradisum" floats. Kiko's song isn't
about damnation; it's about a lonely figure finding peace under the moon. Fauré's
restraint is the model for the verses.

**Orchestration detail to steal:** Fauré's original orchestration OMITTED violins
entirely — strings are violas, cellos, and basses only, plus a solo violin in one
movement. The result is warm, dark, and intimate. He also uses harp extensively
for a shimmering, otherworldly quality. **For Kiko:** add harp arpeggios (broken
Dm9 – Bbmaj9 – Gm9 – A7 figures) under the piano in Verse 1 and the bridge.
Add a solo viola (not violin) doubling Kiko's vocal line at the octave in the
pre-chorus — Fauré's dark string color, not a bright solo violin.

**Chord idea:** Fauré loves maj7 and 9th colors on minor chords.
**Additive layer:** Bbmaj9 (Bb-D-F-A-C) in the new harp + viola layers during
the pre-chorus lift — luminous against the piano's plain Bb triad.

### MUSICAL THEATER

#### 5. "Memory" — *Cats* (Andrew Lloyd Webber, 1981)
**Why it fits:** THE moonlight song. "All alone in the moonlight, I can dream of
the old days." Grizabella, a outcast, alone at night, singing to the moon about
what she lost. Kiko on the pier IS Grizabella on the junkyard tire — a solitary
figure performing for no one under the moon.

**Orchestration detail to steal:** "Memory" builds from a solo voice + sparse piano
to a full theatrical belt with strings, brass, and key change. The orchestration
tells you how alone she is at the start (nothing but piano) and how big her
inner life is by the end (everything). **For Kiko:** keep Verse 1 nearly bare
(piano + harp + solo cello only — no new strings yet). Let the orchestration
accumulate: pre-chorus adds viola + bassoon, chorus 1 adds divisi strings,
chorus 2 adds brass, final chorus adds everything + organ. The arrangement IS
the character arc.

**Chord idea:** "Memory"'s climactic key change lifts everything a step.
**Additive layer:** Kiko's song stays in D minor throughout (no modulation — the
stillness is the point), BUT the new layers can imply lift through voicing:
in the final chorus, voice the new strings in a higher octave with 9ths and
13ths (Dm13: D-F-A-C-E-G) for a "lifting without modulating" effect.

#### 6. "The Music of the Night" — *The Phantom of the Opera* (Lloyd Webber, 1986)
**Why it fits:** Nocturnal, theatrical, seductive darkness. The Phantom sings
about the power of night the way Kiko sings about the moon — it's a love song
to darkness itself. Both characters are masked figures who prefer the night
to the day.

**Orchestration detail to steal:** The original orchestration uses a large string
section with prominent cello, plus synthesized textures and a rock rhythm section
that stays restrained. The darkness comes from low strings + minor key + slow
tempo, not from volume. **For Kiko:** add a low cello/bass pedal (sustained D,
then C#, then D — a slow heartbeat) under the entire song from the pre-chorus
onward. Felt more than heard. This is the "night" under Kiko's feet.

**Chord idea:** The Phantom's harmony uses chromatic mediants (unexpected
third-related chord shifts) for a hypnotic effect. **Additive layer:** in the
bridge, have the new low strings slip from Gm to Ebmaj7 (chromatic mediant)
and back — a brief, dreamlike detour that the piano doesn't follow. Kiko's
vocal stays on the original chords; the bass dreams underneath him.

#### 7. "Empty Chairs at Empty Tables" — *Les Misérables* (Schönberg, 1980)
**Why it fits:** THE empty-room song. Marius alone in the café where his friends
died, singing to empty chairs. "The theater is empty. Encore anyway." is Kiko's
version of this exact sentiment — performing for the absent.

**Orchestration detail to steal:** The song is staged as a solo with minimal
accompaniment that grows as Marius's grief grows. In the original London
orchestration, it opens with solo guitar/piano and builds to full orchestra
for the final "empty chairs at empty tables" reprise. The key device: the
accompaniment DROPS OUT entirely for the most devastating line, leaving the
voice naked. **For Kiko:** in the bridge ("I wonder if father's watching"),
drop ALL new layers — back to just the original piano + strings + choir pad,
or even piano alone for two bars. Then bring everything back for the final
chorus. The silence before the swell is the most powerful orchestration choice.

**Chord idea:** Schönberg uses suspended chords (sus4 resolving to major) for
ache. **Additive layer:** in the pre-chorus, have the new violins play Asus4
(A-D-E) resolving to A major over the piano's plain A triad — a catch in the
throat, harmonically.

### FILM SCORES

#### 8. *Interstellar* — Hans Zimmer (2014)
**Why it fits:** Cosmic loneliness. A father separated from his child by impossible
distance, communicating across time. Kiko wondering "if father's watching" under
the moon is the same ache — separation, longing, love transmitted across a void.
The score's emotional engine is minimalism + massive scale.

**Orchestration detail to steal:** Zimmer's signature is the PIPE ORGAN as an
emotional instrument — not for church grandeur but for lonely vastness. The organ
enters quietly and grows until it fills the universe. Combined with ticking
rhythms (time passing) and string ostinatos. **For Kiko:** add a soft pipe organ
pedal (16' + 8' stops, D minor) entering in Chorus 2 (1:36) and growing through
the final chorus. This is the memorial weight — the "cathedral" under Kiko's
pier. Also: a very soft, slow ticking (like a clock, 60 BPM = 1 tick/second,
matching the tempo) in the verses — time passing while Kiko sings to the moon.

**Chord idea:** Zimmer builds harmony through layering — each new layer adds one
more chord tone until a simple triad becomes a massive cluster. **Additive layer:**
start the final chorus with the piano's Dm triad, then add E (9th) in violins,
then C (7th) in violas, then G (11th) in horns, then B (13th) in organ —
building Dm13 one tone at a time over 8 bars. The chord "grows" the way the
song's emotion grows.

#### 9. "One Summer's Day" — Joe Hisaishi (*Spirited Away*, 2001)
**Why it fits:** Japanese. Piano-led. Wistful. A child navigating a strange world
alone — and finding beauty in it. Hisaishi is Japan's most beloved film composer,
and his voice is the sound of Japanese melancholy. For a song about a Japanese
character (Kiko/Mutoh), this is the cultural anchor.

**Orchestration detail to steal:** Hisaishi's signature is piano + solo violin
or cello + strings, with melodies that use the Japanese *in* scale (D-Eb-G-A-Bb
in D — the "miyako-bushi" sound). He also uses celesta and music-box textures
for childlike wonder. **For Kiko:** add a celesta doubling the piano's D5
water-drop figure in the intro and bridge — a music-box glint, like moonlight
on water. In Verse 2, have the solo cello phrase using the miyako-bushi
inflection (Eb and Bb grace notes around D) — a Japanese melodic fingerprint
inside the Western D minor harmony.

**Chord idea:** Hisaishi often harmonizes minor melodies with major 7th chords
for bittersweetness. **Additive layer:** in Verse 2, have the new harp play
Dm(maj7) arpeggios (D-F-A-C#) under the piano's Dm — the C# against the D minor
key is the "beautiful ache" of Japanese ballad harmony.

## PART 3: Recommended Expanded Orchestration (ADDITIVE ONLY)

### Design principles
1. **Nothing existing changes.** Piano, strings, choir pad are locked.
2. **Accumulate, don't substitute.** New layers enter section by section; the arrangement tells Kiko's emotional arc.
3. **Richer chords = extensions in new layers.** The piano keeps its triads; new instruments add 7ths, 9ths, 11ths, 13ths above/below.
4. **Restraint is power.** (Górecki/Barber lesson.) Not every section gets everything. The bridge strips back. The final chorus gets the kitchen sink.
5. **All achievable via MIDI/sample libraries.** No live orchestra needed.

- + Timpani: final hit.
- Then silence. Water. (Barber's "two final chords" → Kiko's one.)
- *Energy: period. End of sentence.*

## PART 4: Top 3 Recommendations (for the owner)

### 1. Solo Cello + Harp in the Verses (the intimate core)
**Steal from:** Fauré (harp shimmer, no violins), Górecki (solo voice over strings), Barber (cello-like string singing).
**What:** A solo cello that sings Kiko's melody back to him, and a harp playing broken 9th-chord arpeggios under the piano. This is the sound of moonlight on water — intimate, Japanese-ballad bittersweet (Hisaishi's Dm(maj7) in Verse 2).
**Why first:** It's the smallest addition with the biggest emotional return. The verses are currently piano + pad; cello + harp makes them *ache* without adding volume.

### 2. Divided Strings + Muted Brass in the Choruses (the theatrical swell)
**Steal from:** Mozart (D minor darkness, no bright winds), Górecki (10-part divisi, trombones used once for thunderclap).
**What:** High violins holding 9th/11th extensions, low cellos/basses on pedal tones, muted horns swelling in Chorus 2, and a single pair of muted trombones that appear ONLY in the final chorus. Restraint makes the brass devastating.
**Why second:** This is what turns the choruses from "big" to "overwhelming." The existing strings are a pad; the divisi gives them *voices*.

### 3. Pipe Organ Pedal + Harmonic Build in the Final Chorus (the memorial weight)
**Steal from:** Zimmer (*Interstellar* — organ as loneliness, harmony built one tone at a time).
**What:** A soft pipe organ pedal enters in Chorus 2 and grows through the final chorus. Over 8 bars, the new layers stack Dm → Dm9 → Dm11 → Dm13, one extension at a time. The chord literally *grows* the way Kiko's emotion grows. Timpani rolls underneath. Tam-tam on the final slam.
**Why third:** This is the dedication-card music. When the card reads "Dedicated to Keiji Mutoh's father," the organ is what makes the room go quiet. It's the memorial.
