# Co-Producer Show Bible — Working Draft

## 1. Series DNA

**Working title:** Co-Producer

**Format:** Adult animated comedy / surreal workplace-and-everyday-life series.

**Core promise:** Ordinary people take ordinary problems completely seriously while the world around them behaves just slightly wrong. The humor comes from character logic, awkward pauses, escalation, deadpan reactions, and the gap between what a character expects and what actually happens.

**Animation philosophy:** Deliberately limited animation. Simple figures, bold silhouettes, economical backgrounds, strong posing, and selective movement. The show should look inexpensive by design, not unfinished.

**Tone:** Deadpan, strange, uncomfortable, occasionally chaotic, but character-driven rather than random.

## 2. Originality Guardrails

The inspiration is the efficiency and irreverent spirit of low-budget adult animation. The show must remain its own property: original characters, setting, visual identity, dialogue, stories, props, logos, and recurring jokes. Do not reproduce another show's character designs, catchphrases, scenes, assets, or distinctive compositions.

## 3. Series Engine

Every episode should start with a simple problem that can be understood immediately:

> **Someone wants something small → the easiest solution becomes complicated → everyone commits to the wrong solution → consequences escalate → the ending lands on a character choice rather than a random gag.**

This engine lets episodes remain inexpensive while still feeling like complete stories.

## 4. Main Cast — Starter Roster

### The Protagonist

A low-key, practical person who believes every problem has a simple solution. Their greatest flaw is refusing to admit when the solution has stopped being simple.

**Animation requirements:** neutral, annoyed, confused, confident, alarmed, exhausted.

### The Instigator

A friend/coworker who treats every mundane situation like an opportunity. They are confident enough to make bad ideas sound reasonable.

**Animation requirements:** neutral, excited, conspiratorial, defensive, smug, surprised.

### The Straight-Man

The character most capable of recognizing reality — and least capable of getting anyone else to care. Their reactions provide the audience's anchor.

**Animation requirements:** neutral, deadpan, concerned, irritated, disbelief, resignation.

### The Wild Card

A recurring character whose behavior is internally consistent but impossible for everyone else to predict. They should create story turns without functioning as a pure random-joke machine.

**Animation requirements:** neutral, blank, delighted, intense, confused, sudden-action pose.

> Names, final designs, relationships, voices, and backstories remain open for development.

## 5. Visual System

### Character construction

Build characters from reusable components:

- head
- eyes
- mouth
- body
- arms
- hands
- legs
- optional accessories

Prefer vector or clean transparent assets so poses can be recombined without redrawing the character.

### Pose library

Each character starts with six core poses:

1. neutral
2. talking
3. pointing
4. reaction
5. walking
6. exaggerated/emergency

Additional poses are created only when a scene demonstrates that reuse is insufficient.

### Background library

Start with five reusable environment categories:

- home/apartment
- workplace
- street/exterior
- convenience/retail location
- deliberately empty/abstract space

Each location should support multiple camera crops and prop swaps.

## 6. Comedy Grammar

### Timing

- Hold longer than feels natural when the silence is funny.
- Use fast motion only when it creates contrast.
- Let reactions finish before cutting away.
- Do not animate every line.

### Escalation

The first problem should be believable. Each escalation should logically follow from the previous bad decision.

### Visual jokes

Favor jokes that can be executed with a pose, prop, camera change, or background swap rather than a new animation sequence.

### Sound

Silence is an active production tool. A pause, room tone, footstep, click, buzz, or poorly timed sound can replace expensive animation.

## 7. Episode Architecture

### Short-form: 60–90 seconds

- 0:00–0:10 setup
- 0:10–0:30 problem
- 0:30–0:55 escalation
- 0:55–1:15 consequence
- 1:15–1:30 tag/button

### Segment: 3–5 minutes

- cold open
- premise
- two escalation beats
- reversal
- payoff
- short tag

### Standalone: 11–15 minutes

Use an A-story plus a lighter B-story that intersect near the end. Both stories should share assets where possible.

## 8. Production Rules

1. Never animate before the animatic works.
2. Reuse before creating new assets.
3. A new asset must justify its production cost.
4. Record final dialogue early enough to time animation against real performances.
5. Build sound into the animatic, not only at the end.
6. Keep source assets separate from rendered delivery files.
7. Every completed scene gets documented as reusable production knowledge.

## 9. Pilot Development Target

**Pilot prototype:** a 60–90 second standalone scene that introduces the visual grammar without requiring extensive lore.

The first prototype should deliberately test:

- four characters or fewer
- one primary location
- one prop that can be reused
- dialogue-driven performance
- one physical escalation
- one camera gag
- one sound-driven joke
- a clean final button

## 10. Success Test

The production system succeeds if a second 60–90 second scene can be made substantially faster than the first without the second scene looking identical. Reuse should accelerate production while the writing and staging keep the show fresh.
